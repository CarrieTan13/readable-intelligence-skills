# Editorial checklist

This checklist is self-contained. The editor applies it in Stage 2 and again in Stage 5; the independent reviewer applies it under `agents/checklist-reviewer.md` without access to the other files of the skill. It covers problems inherited from the baseline as well as problems introduced by revision; inheritance never makes a problem acceptable.

Each item states what to look for, gives a schematic example, and states when the item is addressed. The "Addressed when" sentence is the complete criterion: judge the revised manuscript against it directly. Examples are schematic; their symbols, labels, and section numbers are placeholders and describe no particular paper. The editor may retain a flagged passage when changing it would alter the mathematics; such a retention appears as a recorded disposition, and the reviewer still reports the problem.

A finding needs a located passage. Frequency scans, such as counts of sentence-initial "No" or "Only", runs of imperatives, qualifier frequency per thousand words, coined-term counts, or statement length, locate candidates but are not findings by themselves. When an item's problem occurs at least about ten times, or in at least three sections, report it once as a pervasive finding with the detection method used, at least three locations in different sections, and an estimate of the number of occurrences.

## A. Formal statements

- **A1 Bundled statement.** Look for a numbered statement with several conclusions that later text cites separately, often a preliminary result collecting unrelated tools. Example: Lemma 3.2 asserts a weighted norm bound, an inequality of a different type, a tail estimate, and an identity for a transform, and later sections cite "the tail estimate in Lemma 3.2" and "the identity in Lemma 3.2". Addressed when each independently cited result is its own numbered statement with its own hypotheses, and every later citation points to the part it uses; or when the parts are so interdependent that they form one result.
- **A2 Commentary inside a statement.** Look for usage advice, motivation, comparisons, or remarks on what is not asserted, inside the statement environment. Example: a lemma ending "This pointwise bound, not the averaged one, is what controls the boundary terms later; no bound on the dependence on k is asserted." Addressed when the statement contains only hypotheses, quantifiers, conclusions, constants, dependencies, and order conditions, the latter in precise form; and the removed commentary is either deleted or placed at its point of use, in a Remark immediately after the statement, or, for justifications, in the proof.
- **A3 Justification inside a statement.** Look for "this follows from", "as verified below", or "by the argument above" within a statement. Example: "Proposition 4.1. … The series converges absolutely; this follows from (2.4) and partial summation." Addressed when the statement only asserts and the justification is in the proof.
- **A4 Imprecise conclusion.** Look for qualitative or accounting verbs in place of a quantified conclusion ("costs a power of log X", "is harmless", "is negligible"), or a proof that ends by proving "the stated interpretation". Example: "Consequently, passing to a supremum over t in an interval of length R costs at most a factor of R", where the proof establishes an explicit inequality. Addressed when the statement asserts the precise inequality, identity, or property that its proof establishes.
- **A5 Context-dependent statement.** Look for statements that open with "Retain the setting above", refer to "the construction just given" or "the setup above", or use objects named only in preceding prose. Example: "Lemma 5.4. Retain the setting above. Then the sum is O(N^{1/2})." becomes "Lemma 5.4. Let w and S be as in Definition 5.1, and assume (5.3). Then …". Addressed when the statement names its objects and assumptions, directly or by explicit reference to numbered definitions, displays, or results.
- **A6 Implicit standing hypothesis.** Look for results stated "under the supposition", results that call a hypothesis "the contradiction", and results valid only because of a hypothesis fixed many pages earlier and never cited in the statement. Example: "Suppose for contradiction that θ > θ₀" at the start of a part, followed by lemmas that use θ > θ₀ without saying so. Addressed when the hypothesis is a labeled display and each statement that depends on it cites it as a hypothesis ("Assume (H).").
- **A7 Quantifiers in procedural language.** Look for quantifier order expressed through "can be chosen independently of", "fixed beforehand", or "chosen later" instead of quantifiers. Example: "Suppose that numbers a, b with 0 < a < 1 can be chosen independently of x. For every x, suppose …" becomes "Suppose there exist a ∈ (0, 1) and b > 0 such that, for every x, …". Addressed when the order is expressed by the quantifiers of the statement.
- **A8 Oversized statement.** Look for a statement in theorem style that runs for more than about a page or introduces its own auxiliary definitions. Example: a proposition whose italic body runs for several pages and defines its own auxiliary functions and enumerated properties. Addressed when the statement is no longer than its assertion requires, the definitions it needs precede it in prose or in a definition, and A1–A3 hold for it.
- **A9 Nested prerequisite burden.** Look for a statement that cites a named setting correctly but still requires several layers of definitions to identify its objects and active assumptions. Example: "For the admissible data of Proposition 4.2", when that proposition itself invokes two earlier constructions. Addressed when a reader can identify the objects and the hypotheses in force from the statement, a brief explanation next to it, and precise references, without reconstructing the chain of earlier setups.
- **A10 Trivial split part.** Look for a numbered statement, created by splitting or promotion, whose entire proof is one or two lines and which is used only together with one other statement. Example: a numbered lemma whose whole proof is "Since the summands are nonnegative, the sum is at least its first term", used only in the proof of the next lemma. Addressed when such content is an item or sentence of the statement it serves, or a sentence of an adjacent remark.
- **A11 Title that does not match the content.** Look for a statement title that describes material outside the statement, often after a split, or that fits only one of several parts. Example: a lemma titled "(Bounds uniform in the parameters)" whose statement is a single inequality for one fixed parameter, with the uniformity discussed in a paragraph after the proof. Addressed when each title describes the content of its own statement in standard terms, or the statement has no title.
- **A12 Relocated commentary that still reads as instructions.** Look for paragraphs between formal statements or appended to proofs, and sentences inside proofs, that tell the reader how a result must be used later ("to use this bound, first …, then …") rather than state mathematics, often left behind when commentary was moved out of a statement. Example: after a lemma, a free paragraph "To use this later, apply it to each function separately and add the results; the hypotheses must also be checked for the derivatives." Addressed when each such passage is at the point of use or in a Remark, states the relevant fact or inequality as mathematics, and contains no instruction-list phrasing; or has been deleted because no later argument needs it.

## B. Granularity and structure

- **B1 Long proof without structure.** Look for proofs of several pages without stage headings or intermediate statements, and for claims proved inside one proof and cited elsewhere. Example: a twelve-page proof containing a bound, proved on its third page, that later sections cite as "the estimate in the proof of Proposition 4.1". Addressed when a reader can see the proof's stages from headings or transitions, and every claim cited outside the proof is a numbered statement or has another stable reference.
- **B2 Unbalanced sectioning.** Look for a section containing only a statement and a pointer to its proof elsewhere, and for a subsection many times longer than its siblings despite natural divisions. Example: a short section that only states a theorem proved several sections later, beside an undivided subsection several times longer than any other. Addressed when sections and subsections correspond to coherent units of the argument and a long unit with natural divisions is subdivided.
- **B3 Disproportionate proof of a standard fact.** Look for full proofs of textbook facts in the main line of the argument. Example: a page proving an elementary inequality found in standard textbooks, inside a section whose purpose is a new estimate. Addressed when such a proof is in an appendix with every step retained, or is short enough not to interrupt the main line.
- **B4 Consequences far from the main theorem.** Example: a corollary of the main theorem placed after a multi-page overview under an unnumbered heading. Addressed when the consequences follow the theorem or are pointed to immediately after it.
- **B5 Overview and roadmap problems.** Look for an overview of several pages with intermediate parameter values and case distinctions, roadmaps at several levels repeating one another, and overview items that cannot be understood without later constructions. Example: the introduction, the opening of a part, and a section introduction each give the same multistage account with the same details. Addressed when there is one roadmap whose items state each stage's purpose and principal result at a consistent level, and section introductions give only local orientation.
- **B6 Missing proof guidance.** Look for long or dense proofs that do not say at their opening how they work, stage headings whose scope does not match the text that follows, and opening sentences that only announce that a result will be proved. Example: "We now prove Lemma 7.2." followed by eight pages without a statement of the mechanism. Addressed when each such proof begins with a sentence or short paragraph stating its mechanism and stages, and each stage heading governs exactly the text up to the next heading of the same level.
- **B7 Delayed assembly of the main argument.** Look for a main theorem whose deduction from existing intermediate results becomes visible only after lengthy technical development, despite an earlier roadmap. Addressed when the existing deduction, or a prose account of its implication chain naming the results used, appears where it orients the reader before the technical proofs, without a duplicated formal result.

## C. Defensive and negative exposition

- **C1 Disclaimers of non-use.** Look for sentences such as "No … is assumed (used, imposed, required, asserted)", "X is not an input", "this uses only …, not …", or "this does not use Y", especially at the start of a sentence. Example: "No independence of the error terms is assumed." "This step does not use Lemma 6.2." Addressed when such sentences are removed, or replaced by one positive statement at the place where a specific misreading would otherwise arise; negations that are part of a mathematical statement or restriction remain.
- **C2 Repeated reassurance about a convention.** Look for repeated statements that a convention fixed once still holds. Example: after defining f to vanish outside a set E, later sections repeatedly remark that f still vanishes outside E. Addressed when the convention is stated once and recalled only where a reader would otherwise have to look far back for it.
- **C3 Review-response residue.** Look for answers to objections the text never raises, sentences insisting that a word or assumption matters, and lists of edge cases that defend a claim rather than treat a case of the proof. Example: after a lemma, "The assumption that the family is bounded matters here; an unbounded family would not do." Addressed when the substance appears positively, as a hypothesis or as a remark explaining why a condition is needed with a supported example, and the defensive sentence is gone.
- **C4 Out-of-scope disclaimers.** Example: "The theorem does not prove Conjecture Y, which remains open [web page]." Addressed when the sentence is removed unless a concrete misreading is likely, and any retained status claim cites a scholarly source.
- **C5 Defensive contrast templates.** Look for "not merely", "X is not Y; it is Z", "rather than" clauses correcting a misconception the text never states, and "clearly" or "obviously" in place of an explanation. Example: "The bound is not a heuristic; it is a theorem." Addressed when each such construction is replaced by a direct positive statement, or is kept only where it corrects a misconception that the text itself has raised.

## D. Terminology and wording

- **D1 Coined jargon.** Look for project-internal nouns, labels, and classifying adjectives used as if standard, especially frequent ones. Example: auxiliary indices called "lanes", a lower bound called a "beacon", the end of an induction called its "harvest". Addressed when each such term is replaced by a standard term or a short descriptive phrase; or, where neither exists, names a mathematical object, is defined in one sentence at its first use, and is used repeatedly and consistently.
- **D2 Jargon in titles.** Look for section and statement titles made of coined terms, or titles that describe a role in the argument rather than mathematical content. Example: "Lemma 7.3 (The gate estimate)" or a proposition titled "(Final bookkeeping)" becomes "Lemma 7.3 (Lower bound for the averaged sum)". Addressed when titles describe the mathematics in standard terms or are omitted.
- **D3 Terms before their definitions.** Look for an abstract, introduction, or overview that relies on terms defined only later. Example: an overview stating that "each beacon above the threshold produces two lanes" before either term is defined. Addressed when the passage uses ordinary mathematical language or explains the term where it appears.
- **D4 Informal type markers.** Look for adjectives used to distinguish objects, such as "the true sum", "the honest identity", or "the naive bound". Addressed when the objects are named or the distinction is stated.
- **D5 Accounting metaphors.** Look for "costs", "budget", "spends", "allowance", "capacity", or "leaves room" in place of inequalities. Example: "this step costs X^ε and leaves a budget of δ/4" becomes "this step loses a factor X^ε, so the total exponent is at most −δ/4 + ε". Addressed when the inequality is stated; a metaphor is kept only if it is defined once and used consistently.
- **D6 Redundant qualifiers.** Look for qualifiers that the conventions already supply, such as "fixed", "exact", "precise", "uniform", "explicit", or "actual", used at high frequency. Example: "Let A be a fixed finite set of fixed functions and fix a fixed constant c", when the conventions make all such data independent of the main parameter, becomes "Let A be a finite set of functions and c > 0". Addressed when each remaining qualifier carries information not already supplied by a stated convention, throughout the manuscript.
- **D7 Assurances and grammar.** Look for missing articles, unclear antecedents of "this" or "it", agreement errors, misnamed hypotheses, and connectives used in the wrong sense, such as "for example" introducing the only argument given. Example: "the proof of existence of solution" becomes "the proof of the existence of a solution". Addressed when the sentence is grammatical and every pronoun has a clear antecedent.
- **D8 Stacked modifiers and overloaded sentences.** Look for necessary but densely nested qualifications, long noun phrases, or a single sentence carrying the setup, several operations, and the conclusion. Example: "the retained normalized conditional measure" when the reader must reconstruct the order of restriction, normalization, and conditioning. Addressed when the subject, the operations in their order, and the scope of each condition are clear, with every mathematical qualification preserved; deleting a necessary qualifier is not a remedy.
- **D9 Cumulative vocabulary burden.** Look for a manuscript that requires the reader to hold many project-specific terms at once, or whose revision uses retained coinages more often than the baseline did. Example: more than a dozen coined labels in active use across the main sections, several of which have standard equivalents. Addressed when each coinage still in use meets the criterion in D1, and either no more than about ten remain in active use or each one beyond ten is a term whose replacement by a standard term or short phrase would make many sentences or formulas materially longer.

## E. Narrative mode

- **E1 Proofs written as instruction lists.** Look for runs of sentence-initial imperatives that perform reasoning steps, such as "Apply", "Insert", "Sum", "Interchange", "Enlarge", and "Bound", without stating what each step establishes, and for "Only now …" and "Only afterward …". Imperatives that introduce objects or fix choices ("Let", "Fix", "Define", "Put", "Set", "Write", "Choose", "Consider", "Denote") are not part of this item. Example: "Apply Hölder's inequality. Only now interchange the two sums. Only afterward insert the bound for the inner integral." becomes "By Hölder's inequality, …. Both sums now converge absolutely, so they may be interchanged; inserting the bound for the inner integral gives …". Addressed when, throughout the manuscript, each step states what it establishes and why it holds.
- **E2 Scattered order of choices.** Look for many remarks such as "fixed before N is chosen", "chosen before x", or "may depend on it" spread across sections with no single explicit statement of the order. Example: parameters ε, δ, N, and X₀ whose order appears only in parenthetical remarks across several sections. Addressed when the order is stated once, through quantifiers, a short list, or a dedicated passage, and referenced; reminders remain only where a reader would otherwise have to look far back.
- **E3 Unexplained constants.** Look for numerical constants and thresholds introduced without the constraint they satisfy. Example: "Take c = 0.37 and κ = 1/9," with no indication of which inequalities require these values. Addressed when the constraint each constant satisfies is stated and the choice is identified as forced or merely convenient.
- **E4 Narration about the document.** Look for sentences that describe the document instead of the mathematics, such as "This section introduces no separate theorem" or "We now turn to the next step", and for transitions without mathematical content. Addressed when each transition states a mathematical goal, change of assumptions, or relation, and contentless announcements are gone.
- **E5 Changes of representation.** Look for a passage where the reader must reconstruct successive restrictions, normalizations, coordinate changes, or returns to original variables from dispersed remarks. Addressed when each difficult transition states what changes, what stays fixed, the normalization involved, and how the conclusion transfers back, including measures, domains, conditioning, and transfer factors.
- **E6 Internal estimates connected only by references.** Look for "combining (3.4), (4.2), and Lemma 5.1 gives …" when the quantity being controlled or the operation combining the estimates is not locally clear. Addressed when the passage states which input controls which term, or shows the decisive substitution, so that the combination can be checked locally.

## F. Redundancy and notation

- **F1 Repeated definitions.** Look for the same object, symbol, or convention defined in several places. Example: the ambient ring defined in the introduction, again in Section 3, and again in Section 6; a normalizing function defined in Section 2 and redefined in Section 7. Addressed when each object is defined once, at or before its first substantive use, and later occurrences are absent or are short reminders marked as such ("recall that …").
- **F2 Repeated explanations.** Look for the same remark in the overview, after a theorem, and in a section introduction. Example: three passages explaining why an auxiliary family is needed. Addressed when the explanation appears once, where it does useful work, and other places refer to it or are silent.
- **F3 Notation aliases.** Look for two symbols declared to denote one object. Example: "We also write 𝒜 = A." Addressed when one symbol remains everywhere and the declaration is gone.
- **F4 Alias chains and one-use names.** Look for chains of renamings, symbols that abbreviate a simple expression used once or twice, and parameters introduced only to be specialized immediately. Example: "Put B = A², and let C = B/2; then C ≤ 1" where C is never used again. Addressed when each remaining name identifies a meaningful object, exposes a dependency, or organizes a substantial calculation.
- **F5 Symbol collisions and decorations.** Look for one letter used for unrelated objects within the same argument, and for decorations (subscripts, primes, tildes, fonts) whose distinction is unexplained. Example: k denoting both an index of summation and a fixed field in the same proof. Addressed when, within each argument, distinct objects have distinct symbols and every decoration's distinction is explained at its introduction.
- **F6 Dense groups of necessary notation.** Look for several distinct families of constants, indices, or auxiliary quantities introduced together without a usable grouping by role. Addressed when readers can distinguish the main quantity, the governing parameters, and the supporting controls near their use, with all conditions preserved.
- **F7 Repeated mathematical exposition.** Look for a derivation or detailed setup repeated after its complete treatment elsewhere in the same setting. Addressed when the repetition is replaced by a precise reference with any useful short reminder, or when distinct hypotheses, cases, or normalizations make the second treatment necessary.

## G. Front matter and introduction

- **G1 Abstract.** Look for an abstract that restates the theorem without scope or method, or is disproportionate to the paper. Example: a two-sentence abstract for a long paper. Addressed when the abstract gives the main result, its scope, and the principal method in a few sentences.
- **G2 Encyclopedic history.** Look for a chronological account of the field's classical milestones before the problem is stated. Addressed when historical context is limited to the lineage that leads to the present difficulty.
- **G3 Prior work as a citation list.** Look for sentences that list cited results with locators and give no account of achievements and obstacles. Example: "We use [A, Theorem 2], [B, Section 3], [C, Lemma 4], and [D, (1.1)]." Addressed when the prior-work discussion explains what earlier work achieved, what obstacle remained, and how the present argument relates to it, and detailed dependency information appears where each result is applied.
- **G4 Over-fine locators in the introduction.** Example: "[7, p. 12, display (3) and the remark after it]" in a historical sentence. Addressed when detailed locators appear where the result is applied.
- **G5 Title and metadata.** Look for the paper's title reused as the title of a part or section, and for missing subject classification codes and keywords. Addressed when the paper, its parts, and its sections have distinct titles, and the front matter gives Mathematics Subject Classification codes and keywords.
- **G6 Inaccessible central mechanism.** Look for an introduction from which the obstacle, the central idea, and the significance cannot be recovered without later definitions. Addressed when the introduction states, in ordinary mathematical language, the obstacle, the decisive construction or observation, why it overcomes the obstacle, and the significance of the result.

## H. Citations and bibliography

- **H1 Access notes in entries.** Example: entries ending "author's copy", "publisher PDF", or "version consulted via a mirror". Addressed when no entry contains notes about how a source was accessed.
- **H2 Duplicate or inconsistent identifiers.** Example: the same preprint identifier given twice in one entry; identifiers present for some entries and absent from comparable ones. Addressed when each entry has at most one identifier of each kind and the entries share one format.
- **H3 Unstable or internal identifiers.** Example: an internal release code or a search-result URL where a stable bibliographic reference to the same work exists. Addressed when entries use stable bibliographic references wherever one exists for the same work.
- **H4 Tangential source for a standard fact.** Example: a classical identity cited to a recent paper on an unrelated application. Addressed when standard facts are cited to standard sources.
- **H5 Web page cited for a mathematical claim.** Example: an institutional web page cited for the status of a conjecture. Addressed when mathematical claims cite scholarly sources.
- **H6 Uncited entries and unresolved citations.** Look for bibliography entries cited nowhere in the text, including entries whose only citation disappeared in revision, and for citations that do not resolve to an entry. Example: an entry whose only citation was in a sentence removed during revision. Addressed when every entry is cited and every citation resolves.

## I. Typography and cross-references

- **I1 Cross-reference style.** Look for "Equation (2.3)" capitalized in mid-sentence and mixed with "(2.3)", and for sentences that begin with a bare equation number. Addressed when one style is used throughout, typically "(2.3)" or "equation (2.3)" in mid-sentence, and no sentence begins with a bare number.
- **I2 Mixed numbering schemes for displays.** Example: displays tagged (I), (II), and (★) among numbered displays and referenced from other subsections. Addressed when displays use one numbering scheme and any local tags are confined to, and referenced only within, a single proof.
- **I3 Duplicate titles.** Example: two subsections in different parts with the same title. Addressed when titles are distinct and descriptive.
- **I4 Editorial residue and process traces.** Look for edition banners, references to a supplied file, audit markers, expert-review flags, workflow language, renaming commentary, and access notes anywhere in the manuscript, including captions and footnotes. Addressed when the manuscript contains none of these; a legitimate mathematical use of words such as "original" is not residue.
- **I5 Proof layout.** Look for a "Proof." stranded on its own line, standalone bold stage headings, and stage headings with the wrong scope. Addressed when every proof opens with "Proof." followed by text or a run-in heading in the same paragraph, stage headings are run-in, and each governs exactly the text up to the next heading of the same level.
- **I6 Nonstandard numbering of formal statements.** Look for letter suffixes, primes, or "bis" in statement numbers, and for gaps or out-of-order numbers. Example: "Lemma 3.2a", "Lemma 3.2b", and "Proposition 3.2bis" after a split. Addressed when formal statements follow one consecutive numbering scheme and every reference uses the current numbers.
