# Restructuring

This file supports Stages 2–4 of the workflow in `SKILL.md`. The preservation requirements §§P1–P4 in `SKILL.md` take precedence over everything here.

## S1. Introduction and proof roadmap

Keep the main result, its essential motivation, and the obstacle addressed by the proof accessible in the introduction. Preserve useful historical context that leads naturally to the technical problem. Keep it proportionate: the intended reader needs the lineage that leads to the present difficulty, not a chronological survey of the field's classical milestones.

Before the detailed roadmap, explain the central mechanism with minimal notation: the obstacle, the decisive construction or observation, why it addresses the obstacle, and how the other properties needed by the argument are retained. Integrate this explanation into effective existing prose. Do not force a proof with several essential ideas into a misleading single-idea account.

Explain the headline statement in ordinary mathematical language alongside its formal formulation, retaining the quantifier order and qualifications and making its symbols interpretable there. Address specific likely misunderstandings about the direction or type of bound, parameter dependence, constructivity, or computational guarantee. Distinguish an existence result, an effective construction, and a practical implementation when relevant and supported. Do not invent runtime estimates, crossover points, or stronger consequences. A scope clarification should answer a concrete reader question, without imposing a generic list of disclaimers on every paper.

Assess whether the existing deduction of the main theorem from the main intermediate results should appear before their technical proofs. When the final deduction is buried after extensive machinery, moving that deduction earlier can explain why the later results suffice. Name the existing results being used, preserve their prerequisites and the order of choices, and locate their deferred proofs. If moving the deduction would require premature notation or duplicate the argument, keep it in place and give a short supported prose account at the earlier point. Do not add a new summary proposition or a second full proof. A short paper whose closing deduction is readily accessible may need no such change.

Give the reader one usable account of the main proof route. For a multistage argument whose connections are difficult to retrieve from prose, use a compact numbered roadmap. Each item should state the step's mathematical purpose and identify its principal existing result and section. Do not turn the roadmap into a catalogue of every lemma.

Keep roadmap stages at a consistent conceptual level: their purpose, the reason they work, and how they enable the next stage. Introduce detailed sampling laws, auxiliary coordinates, and intermediate calculations near their substantive use unless they are essential to the introductory explanation. Preserve relocated material and its prerequisites. A numbered list is still too demanding if understanding each item requires learning several later constructions at once.

Integrate the roadmap with the existing overview. It can replace scattered navigational sentences while retaining explanations of why the steps work. Do not add a second full account of the same construction or repeat its central calculation in another introductory or related-work passage.

State important consequences of the main result next to it, or point to them there, rather than after a long overview. The abstract should state the main result and its scope and name the principal method in a few sentences; an abstract that only restates the theorem is insufficient for a long or technical paper. Terms used in the abstract and introduction must be understandable there: use ordinary mathematical language, or explain a technical term where it first appears, rather than relying on definitions given many pages later.

## S2. Construction and section order

Organize constructions around the objects and properties readers need to understand. Identify what is being constructed and what its later use requires before presenting intricate machinery, using existing statements and supported explanations.

Identify the central object using a mathematical category supported by its definition, and explain its inputs, outputs, structure, and purpose as applicable. Distinguish the existing framework from the object constructed here and identify which properties are inherited and which are established in the manuscript. Readers should not have to reconstruct the object's identity from several sections of technical details. Do not adopt a novelty claim or a new technical classification solely from community commentary.

Before a consequential construction or parameter choice, explain the mathematical requirements it must meet and how the choice meets them. Where supported by the supplied argument, identify the specific failure of a simpler choice. Distinguish a convenient sufficient choice from a necessary one. Explain what the main properties of a gadget or intermediate result enable downstream, so readers can understand their purpose before following their proofs.

When abstraction creates a specific obstacle, assess whether a worked instance or comparison figure would resolve it. Prefer an existing example or a directly checked specialization of the supplied mathematics. State the example's limits, preserve the full argument, and do not introduce new formal results. For algorithms, protocols, or decoding arguments, distinguish information available to each participant from witnesses used only in the analysis. Reuse effective figures and explanations; additional examples and diagrams are optional.

Where a construction has several analyses, assess whether completing one conceptual strand before starting another would make the argument easier to follow. For example, a nonlinear construction and its stability analysis may be clearer together, followed by the separate analysis of linear observations. Choose the order from the actual dependencies; this example does not prescribe a universal order.

Keep definitions, constructions, examples, and their immediate uses close. Preserve parameter order, sampling laws, and all assumptions when regrouping material. Do not move prerequisite choices out of scope or introduce symbols in a preview before readers can interpret them.

Use section introductions to resolve actual orientation problems. Reuse or revise an effective existing introduction. Add an introduction when readers otherwise cannot identify the section's goal or connection to the proof. Its length and content should fit that need; no fixed sentence count or mandatory list of inputs, outputs, and downstream sections applies.

Keep whole-paper orientation outside a subsection devoted to one local stage. Avoid creating a section with a solitary numbered subsection; use the parent section or a coherent subdivision when appropriate. Preserve useful navigation categories when changing run-in labels into headings or topic sentences. A heading may lead directly to a subheading without filler prose. Assess inherited section structure in the same way: a section that only states a result proved elsewhere, or a subsection many times longer than its siblings despite natural internal divisions, usually needs merging or subdivision. Give distinct sections distinct titles.

## S3. Long proofs

For a long argument that appears to leave the main proof route, determine whether it supplies a later inference, handles a necessary case, explains a limitation, gives an alternative proof, or establishes an independent result. Check implicit dependencies as well as explicit references; absence of a citation does not establish that a passage is dispensable. Distinguish repeated exposition from distinct mathematical arguments before simplifying it.

Where moving a supporting argument, alternative proof, or exploratory discussion to an appendix improves the main exposition, preserve it in full with its prerequisites, assumptions, labels, and citations. Leave an accurate pointer and explain its role at the point where readers need it; recheck all affected uses and the statement inventory. A deferred necessary proof remains part of the main argument's dependencies. Use descriptive mathematical headings, and keep editorial classifications such as redundancy or a suspected dead end in the ledger. These moves do not permit deleting formal statements or distinct proof content under §§P1–P4.

For every long or technically dense proof, assess whether the reader can anticipate its stages. When that structure is hard to infer, add or revise a brief overview before the proof or at its opening. State the actual mechanism and the role of the stages, using existing notation and results.

A useful overview might explain that the proof first establishes an upper bound, then derives an incompatible lower bound under the contradiction hypothesis. A sentence that merely announces that the lemma will now be proved does not help.

For a proof with several distinct stages, use clear paragraph transitions or short italic run-in stage headings where they reduce cognitive load. Newly added or revised proof-stage headings must be followed by their text in the same paragraph; do not use standalone bold headings or section-level commands to create proof steps. At the opening of a proof, keep "Proof." with the opening text or run-in heading, rather than on a line by itself. Check each heading's scope through the next heading at the same level or the end of the proof. Preserve useful original navigation cues. Keep the calculations and justifications intact. Avoid repeating the overview in each stage, restating the full hypotheses, or paraphrasing an adjacent display without explaining its role.

Within a dense calculation, identify the quantity being estimated and the operation that connects the cited bounds. When several internal results are combined, say which controls which term, or expose the decisive already-supported substitution. A string of equation numbers can locate ingredients without explaining their combination. Add only the explanation needed to follow that step; routine algebra need not acquire a line-by-line commentary. If the inference itself is uncertain, preserve it and record the question under §P4.

When an argument changes coordinates, restricts a measure, conditions a law, rescales an object, or returns to the original setting, check that the reader can follow the current object through that transition. At difficult transitions, state what changes, what remains fixed, the relevant normalization, and the relation needed to transfer the conclusion back. Keep normalizers, conditioning events, information restrictions, and restoration factors explicit where consequential. A short relation or local table may help a complicated sequence, but do not impose one on routine substitutions or repeat the full setting at every step.

When a proof is deferred, state where it appears with a stable section or result reference. A precise page reference is useful only when it resolves a real lookup problem and remains correct after reflow. Do not routinely combine a section reference, a proof-title description, and a page number for the same destination.

## S4. External theorem applications

Review every external application for the information readers need to check it. In the paper, spell out the parameter substitution or hypothesis check when it is consequential: a nontrivial dimension or degree condition, a delicate independence assumption, the order of parameter choices, a quantitative bound used later, or a possible mismatch of settings.

Write the decisive specialization and explain why its conditions hold. Retain enough detail for verification without repeating an external theorem's full hypothesis list at every use. A straightforward later application can refer to an earlier checked specialization.

Omit identity substitutions such as `k = k` or `A = A`. Do not manufacture a proof consisting of identity substitutions and references to results that already state the desired facts. When a numerical or symbolic substitution changes a bound, show the part that matters.

If applicability cannot be established from the supplied or retrieved material, preserve the existing claim and record the exact uncertainty in the report. Do not insert a missing-source disclaimer into an otherwise routine attribution, and do not present an unread result as verified.

## S5. Prior work and applications

Check substantive paraphrases of cited results, including claims in the introduction and historical discussion that are not used as proof inputs. Compare each claim with the cited version and specific result: its objects, hypotheses, quantifiers, and conclusion. Where relevant, check the direction and kind of reduction or equivalence, effectiveness, and uniformity conditions. Distinguish what the cited authors establish from a consequence derived in this manuscript or a historical characterization. A valid citation or matching title does not verify a paraphrase.

Record the manuscript passage, inspected source version and locator, and comparison outcome in the existing ledger. Reuse a checked source comparison for repeated references to the same result while checking each occurrence's wording. When the evidence is unavailable, record the exact missing check and continue independent editorial work. When a paraphrase appears mathematically inaccurate, retain it unchanged and report the discrepancy with its evidence in the change report; do not change its mathematical content or place expert-review flags in the manuscript. A complaint about a citation identifies a check to perform, not a verified error. Perform these comparisons in Stage 2 for the baseline text, and repeat them in Stage 5 for every paraphrase that the revision changed.

Review the substance and placement of comparisons with prior work. Explain the relevant earlier achievement, the remaining obstacle, and the present argument's supported contribution. Compare hypotheses or techniques when that comparison is meaningful and evidenced. Do not force every cited work into a stronger/weaker classification or treat every algorithm or limitation as an apparent contradiction.

A prior-work paragraph should explain what earlier work achieved, what obstacle remained, and how the present argument relates to it. A sequence of citations with detailed locators is a dependency list, not an account. Move detailed locators (page, display, or paragraph numbers) to the point where a result is applied, unless they resolve a genuine ambiguity in the introduction. State limitations that answer a concrete reader question; do not add disclaimers that the result does not settle a famous stronger problem unless a specific misreading is likely, and support any claim about the status of an open problem with a scholarly source rather than a web page.

Distinguish the central construction, adaptations of earlier methods, imported results, and supporting steps through their mathematical roles and supported attribution. Explain what depends on an important lemma, rather than labeling every lemma as essential or routine. Claim novelty only to the extent supported by the supplied or retrieved material. When a specific apparent conflict with a known bound or algorithm is raised, explain the relevant difference in hypotheses, model, or parameter regime if the sources establish it.

When supplied feedback identifies another proof, a follow-up, or a companion paper, first establish its identity, version, scope, and relevance from the supplied or retrieved material. Explain the relationship when it helps readers: dependency, alternative approach, consequence, or improvement. Do not infer equivalence, priority, validity, or independence from a comment or link alone. Record missing evidence as unresolved; neither a new survey section nor network retrieval is required, and §W2 states when retrieval may be used. For formalization questions, distinguish the formal statement, proof-checking evidence, and its correspondence to the manuscript; report only checks actually supported by inspected material.

A separate Related Work section is optional. Keep historical discussion integrated with the introduction when it establishes motivation and leads into the technical difficulty. Keep attribution at the point of method use. Create or expand a separate section only when it improves the manuscript's information flow; do not insert a long literature survey between the proof overview and its conventions by default.

Application statements should remain near their problem definitions and qualifications. Do not duplicate the applications section in Related Work. A short introductory statement of significance can point forward without reproducing thresholds, definitions, and derivations already given in full later.

When significance is hard to recognize, briefly name representative consequences already supported by the manuscript and point to their precise treatment. Retain assumptions, strict thresholds, problem variants, and attribution. This guidance does not permit adding corollaries; bibliography additions follow §P3.

Use only the baseline, the supplied material, and sources retrieved under §W2. An external style corpus is optional and must not become a prerequisite for revision. Do not attribute conventions or claims to sources not actually inspected.

## S6. Formal statements and granularity

Write each formal statement so that a specialist can read and cite it on its own. It should assert one result, or a few tightly connected parts, with explicit hypotheses and a precise conclusion. Keep usage advice, comparisons, and motivation at the point of use or in a Remark immediately after the statement, and keep justifications in the proof, following the classification and order of destinations in §P1. A statement that runs for more than about a page in theorem style, or whose body contains its own definitions, justifications, and advice, is a signal to apply §P1 and §P3, not an acceptable default.

Check the prerequisite chain behind references to a named setting or construction. A correct citation to a definition is insufficient when using it requires recovering several other packages of local terminology. Briefly identify the objects and the properties relevant to this result, or bring the needed setup closer, while retaining precise references and every binding hypothesis. Do not repeat entire definitions in every statement. If a package's content is ambiguous, record the ambiguity without silently selecting an interpretation.

Assess granularity across the manuscript. A preliminary lemma that collects unrelated tools, each cited separately later, is easier to use when split under §P3. A proof that runs for many pages without intermediate statements may need stage headings under §S3, and an intermediate assertion that it proves and other arguments cite may be stated as a lemma under §P3. A standard fact proved at a length disproportionate to its role, such as an elementary inequality or a textbook property of a transform, may have its proof moved to an appendix with every step retained. Never replace the proof by a citation (§W2).

When a long part of the argument operates under a standing hypothesis, such as a contradiction hypothesis, state it once in a labeled display and make each dependent statement cite it explicitly as a hypothesis ("Assume (H)."), rather than relying on phrases such as "under the supposition" or calling the hypothesis "the contradiction". Express quantifier order through the quantifiers of the statement, not through procedural phrases such as numbers that "can be chosen independently" or are "fixed beforehand". Titles of formal statements should describe their mathematical content in standard terms; omit a title rather than use internal jargon.

After a split or a promotion, check every resulting statement. Its number follows the document's ordinary sequence, without letter suffixes (§P3). Its title describes its own content, or it has no title. It is not a one- or two-line consequence that belongs inside the statement it serves. Its hypotheses can be identified from the statement and its explicit references. The commentary removed from it has a destination that satisfies §P1, and the moved text has been rewritten where it read as an instruction list or a usage manual.

A preliminary section that collects tools for later use should state each tool once, in the form in which it is used, and explain where it is used at that point of use rather than in paragraphs between the tools. When several tools share a setting, state the setting once before them, as prose or an existing definition, and let each statement refer to it.
